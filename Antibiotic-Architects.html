      • The test is a molecular dynamics simulation. The computer works out the forces between your molecule and the enzyme, then moves everything a tiny step in time, again and again.
      • Your molecule starts in the pocket, where a docking program would put it. The simulation shows whether it stays there while everything jiggles.

      • In the expert missions, longer molecules hold their bends with springs, and ring parts hang below the molecule. A ring only fits where the pocket has a deep spot for it.
      • For the scientists: 2D Langevin dynamics in reduced units. The enzyme keeps its shape with an elastic network; three pocket residues carry chemistry. Ligand–residue interactions are Lennard-Jones wells whose depth depends on the pair of types, with WCA repulsion otherwise. Occupancy is the fraction of frames where the ligand RMSD from the docked pose is below 0.75σ, pooled over 12 replicas of 500τ. Expert pockets are built around the target pose, with a cavity under each deep spot; each ring is a separate, larger bead on a stalk; bends are signed angle potentials; and each ligand bead feels a short-ranged cosine-squared well only from the residue at its own spot, so every spot counts.

      Tiny machines with pockets

      Every living thing is full of proteins: tiny machines that do almost every job, from carrying oxygen in your blood to copying your DNA. Bacteria are full of them too.

      Many proteins are enzymes, which speed up chemical reactions. Each enzyme has a pocket where the molecule it works on fits snugly, like a key in a lock. Block that pocket with the wrong key and the enzyme stops working.

      For the scientists

      Emil Fischer suggested the lock-and-key picture in 1894. In 1958 Daniel Koshland proposed induced fit: the pocket changes shape as the molecule binds. Proteins are always flexing, which is one reason simulations of their motion are so useful in drug design.

      How antibiotics work

      An antibiotic is a molecule that jams a protein that bacteria need, but that our own cells don't have or can do without. The missions in this game are based on real targets:

      • Cell-wall builders. Penicillin jams the enzymes bacteria use to build their tough outer wall, so the bacteria burst.
      • The DNA untangler. Ciprofloxacin jams DNA gyrase, which bacteria need to untangle their DNA when they copy it.
      • The vitamin maker. Trimethoprim jams the enzyme bacteria use to make a vitamin called folate.
      • The fatty-acid builder. Some new antibiotics jam FabI, an enzyme bacteria use to build their cell membranes.

      Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928, when mould killed bacteria on a dish he'd left out. Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their team turned it into a medicine in the 1940s, and the three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize.

      For the scientists

      Humans have their own dihydrofolate reductase, the vitamin maker, but trimethoprim binds the bacterial enzyme tens of thousands of times more strongly. Small differences in the pocket make that selectivity possible, which is the idea behind the “No side effects” mission.

      Rings, shapes and π interactions

      Many medicines contain flat rings of six carbon atoms, called aromatic rings. Their electrons spread out in clouds above and below the ring. Three amino acids have rings like this too: phenylalanine (Phe), tyrosine (Tyr) and tryptophan (Trp). The expert missions use them.

      • π–π stacking. Two flat rings stack like coins, held together by their electron clouds. (π is the Greek letter pi, which chemists use for these electrons.)
      • Cation–π. Anything positive (a cation) is attracted to the electron cloud on the face of a ring. Tryptophan is especially good at this: more than a quarter of all tryptophans in known protein structures are doing it.
      • Shape complementarity. A molecule can have perfect chemistry and still not fit if it's the wrong shape. Bulky rings need room, and long molecules have to bend the same way as the pocket.

      Shape can make a drug selective, too. In the AcrB pump of E. coli, an experimental pump blocker slots into a narrow pit lined with phenylalanines and stacks its ring against one of them. In a related pump, MexY, a bulkier tryptophan sits in the same place, and the blocker can't bind.

      For the scientists

      Aromatic rings in proteins usually stack offset (parallel-displaced) or edge-to-face (T-shaped) rather than exactly face-to-face. Among the aromatic amino acids, tryptophan takes part in cation–π interactions most often (Gallivan and Dougherty, PNAS 1999), and nicotine binds its receptor in the brain through a cation–π interaction with a tryptophan (Xiu et al., Nature 2009). The AcrB and MexY example is from Nakashima et al. (Nature 2013), with the inhibitor D13-9001. In the game, π–π stacking and cation–π are simply the strongest pair types, and rings are larger beads that only fit cavities in the pocket.

      Testing with molecular dynamics

      Before spending weeks making a molecule in the lab, scientists can test it on a computer. A docking program quickly guesses how the molecule might sit in the pocket, like a photo. Molecular dynamics, or MD, then makes a movie: it shows whether the molecule stays put while everything wobbles and jiggles.

      Each step moves time forward by only about 2 femtoseconds. A femtosecond is a millionth of a billionth of a second: there are about as many femtoseconds in one second as there are seconds in 32 million years.

      How much simulated time do you want?

      Because the jiggling is random, one simulation can be lucky or unlucky. So scientists run several copies, called replicas, and average them. The game does the same: every test runs 12 replicas at once.

      For the scientists

      The forces come from a force field such as AMBER, CHARMM or GROMOS: terms for bonds, angles and torsions, plus electrostatics and van der Waals interactions between every pair of nearby atoms, usually with thousands of explicit water molecules. In drug design, MD is used to check docked poses (often by tracking ligand RMSD, as this game does), to estimate binding free energies with methods such as free energy perturbation, to measure how long a drug stays bound (its residence time), and to find “cryptic” pockets that only open as the protein moves.

      The first MD simulation of a protein (McCammon, Gelin and Karplus, 1977) covered less than 10 picoseconds. In 2013 Martin Karplus, Michael Levitt and Arieh Warshel shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for multiscale models of complex chemical systems.

      This game is a 2D toy: a few pocket amino acids, molecules of three to five parts and simple oily, charged, polar and ring interactions. Real binding depends on 3D shape, hydrogen-bond geometry, water in the pocket and much more.

      Superbugs

      Bacteria can divide every 20 minutes or so, and each time there's a small chance of a mutation. If a mutation changes the drug's pocket so the drug no longer fits, that bacterium survives the antibiotic and passes the change on. This is evolution by natural selection, happening fast enough to watch. A superbug is a bacterium that has become resistant to several antibiotics.

      Bacteria have four main tricks:

      • Change the pocket. In E. coli, swapping a single amino acid in DNA gyrase (serine 83 for leucine) is one of the most common ways to resist ciprofloxacin. The “Superbug!” mission is based on this.
      • Shred the drug. Enzymes called β-lactamases cut penicillin-type antibiotics apart. Co-amoxiclav pairs amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, which blocks the shredder.
      • Keep it out. Bacteria such as E. coli have an extra outer wall that most molecules can't get through. In 2019, chemists found that adding a positively charged amine group helped a drug get in. The drug, Debio-1452-NH3, then worked against these tougher bacteria. The Stansfeld group, who made this game, simulate the proteins that build and maintain this outer wall.
      • Pump it out. Pumps in the bacterial wall push the drug back out before it can work.

      Drug-resistant infections directly caused about 1.14 million deaths worldwide in 2021, and researchers forecast nearly 39 million more between 2025 and 2050 unless we act. New antibiotics are urgently needed, and computer methods like docking, molecular dynamics and AI help find them faster.

      For the scientists

      Mutations that help bacteria escape a drug often make the enzyme work less well, a fitness cost. In the arms race, the bacteria can only make changes that keep their pocket working. Resistance usually builds up step by step: in E. coli, high-level fluoroquinolone resistance often combines GyrA S83L with D87N. The amine design rules for Gram-negative entry are known as the eNTRy rules (Hergenrother lab). Polymyxins, last-line antibiotics, attack the outer membrane itself, and the Stansfeld group studies how bacteria remodel their membranes to resist them. AI models have also found new antibiotic candidates, such as halicin (2020) and abaucin (2023).

      A photo and a movie

      In 2020, the AI program AlphaFold showed it could predict many protein structures from their sequence with remarkable accuracy, and its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. A predicted structure is like a photo. But proteins are never still: they wobble, flex, open and close. Molecular dynamics gives us the movie, showing how a protein moves, how a medicine binds to it, and what changes when a mutation swaps one amino acid for another.

      Help keep antibiotics working

      • Antibiotics don't work for viral infections such as colds and flu, and most coughs.
      • Only take antibiotics prescribed for you, and take them as your doctor or pharmacist tells you.
      • Never share them with anyone else.

      Advice from the NHS. Every unnecessary use of antibiotics gives bacteria another chance to evolve resistance.
