      What's going on in this simulation?
      • Springs hold neighbouring beads together, like the chemical bonds along a real protein chain.
      • Oily beads attract each other a little when they're close. Everything else just bumps off each other.
      • The water is invisible, but it's there: it slows the beads down and gives them random kicks. The hotter it is, the harder the kicks. Scientists call this an implicit solvent with a Langevin thermostat.
      • For the scientists: 2D bead–spring chain, harmonic bonds, weak angle stiffness, Lennard-Jones attraction between H beads and WCA repulsion for all other pairs, BAOAB Langevin integrator, all in reduced units (energies in units of ε, time in τ).

      How this works
      • Each test is a short molecular dynamics run. The molecule starts in the pocket, where a docking program would place it, and the simulation shows whether it stays there while everything jiggles.
      • Every run is a little different because the jiggling is random, so each test quietly runs eight copies of each simulation and averages them. Researchers do the same, and call the copies replicas.
      • The three amino acids lining each pocket decide what sticks: oily with oily, positive with negative, and water-loving parts weakly with charged or water-loving ones. Two of the same charge push apart.
      • You can drag the molecule with your finger to feel how strongly it's held.
      • For the scientists: 2D Langevin dynamics in reduced units. Each enzyme keeps its fold with an elastic network plus weak position restraints; the ligand is a three-bead molecule with an angle term; ligand–protein interactions are Lennard-Jones wells whose depth depends on the pair of types, with WCA repulsion otherwise. Occupancy is the fraction of frames with the ligand's centre within 0.8σ of the docked pose.

      Tiny machines made from a string of beads

      Proteins do almost every job in your body. Haemoglobin carries oxygen in your blood. Enzymes break down your food. Antibodies grab hold of germs. Keratin makes your hair and nails.

      Each protein is a chain of smaller building blocks called amino acids, joined like beads on a string. There are 20 kinds. The order of the beads, called the sequence, is written in your DNA. Once a chain is made, it folds itself up into a precise 3D shape, and that shape decides what the protein can do.

      For the scientists

      In the 1950s and 60s Christian Anfinsen showed that an unfolded enzyme, ribonuclease A, could refold by itself and work again: the sequence alone carries the instructions for the structure. He shared the 1972 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The human genome has around 20,000 protein-coding genes, and a typical protein is a few hundred amino acids long.

      Oil and water

      Shake a jar of oil and water and the oil drops soon find each other again. Some amino acids are oily too. We call them hydrophobic, which means “water-fearing”. In water, a protein folds so that its oily amino acids cluster together in the middle, away from the water, while the polar (water-loving) ones stay on the outside.

      This is the hydrophobic effect, and it's the main force that makes proteins fold. It's exactly what you're doing in the puzzles.

      For the scientists

      Side chains such as leucine, isoleucine, valine and phenylalanine form the cores of most globular proteins. The hydrophobic effect is largely driven by water itself: water molecules next to a nonpolar surface are restricted in how they can hydrogen-bond, so burying that surface increases the entropy of the surrounding water. Hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and van der Waals packing then fine-tune the structure. Folded proteins are only marginally stable, typically by about 20–60 kJ/mol, the equivalent of a handful of hydrogen bonds.

      The rules of the puzzles

      In the snap puzzles, each piece is an amino acid with a side chain that decides what it can stick to. Oily side chains stick together (the hydrophobic effect). A positive and a negative side chain attract each other and form a salt bridge. Two cysteines can join with a disulfide bond, a strong sulfur bridge. Water-loving side chains are happy on the outside, touching the water.

      Real proteins are complicated, so scientists often use simplified models to understand the big ideas. The grid challenge uses the HP model, invented by Ken Dill in 1985. There are only two kinds of bead, H (hydrophobic) and P (polar), and the chain lives on a square grid. Every time two oily beads sit next to each other without being directly joined, that's a contact. Each contact lowers the energy by 1, and the best fold has the lowest energy.

      For the scientists

      Even this toy model is hard. Finding the lowest-energy fold of an HP chain on the 2D square lattice is NP-hard (proved in 1998), so no known method solves every case quickly. The research benchmark levels are standard test sequences from Unger and Moult (1993) that have been used for decades to compare folding algorithms. The best scores shown in this game were checked with an exact computer search for every level up to 25 beads, and match the best scores reported in the literature for the 36- and 48-bead chains.

      One handy rule: the square grid is like a chessboard, so beads that are an even number of places apart along the chain sit on the same colour square and can never touch.

      Too many shapes to try one by one

      Suppose each amino acid could only point in 3 different ways. A chain of 100 would still have 3100 possible shapes. How long would it take to try them all?

      Possible shapes

      Real proteins fold in anything from a few millionths of a second to a few minutes. This puzzle is called Levinthal's paradox (1969). The answer is that proteins don't search randomly. Each small step that tucks away some oil makes the chain a bit more stable, so it gets guided downhill towards the right shape.

      Rolling downhill: the folding funnel

      Scientists picture folding as a ball rolling down a bumpy funnel. Height is energy, and the bottom is the folded shape. The bumps are traps where the chain gets tangled. Temperature is how much the ball jiggles.

      ColdHot

      Try it cold: the ball gets stuck in a bump. Try it hot: it keeps bouncing back out of the bottom. Somewhere in between, it finds the bottom and stays there.

      Molecular dynamics: making movies of molecules

      Molecular dynamics, or MD, is a way of using a computer to watch molecules move. The computer knows where every atom is and how atoms push and pull on each other. Then it repeats the same few steps, over and over:

      Each step moves time forward by only about 2 femtoseconds. A femtosecond is a millionth of a billionth of a second: there are about as many femtoseconds in one second as there are seconds in 32 million years.

      How much simulated time do you want?

      The simulation lab in this game is a real, if tiny, molecular dynamics simulation of a bead chain. Research simulations work the same way, with every atom of the protein and thousands of water molecules around it.

      For the scientists

      The forces come from a force field such as AMBER, CHARMM or GROMOS: terms for bond stretching, angle bending and torsions, plus electrostatics and van der Waals interactions between every pair of nearby atoms. Water is usually modelled explicitly and often makes up most of the atoms. The timestep is limited by the fastest motions, the vibrations of bonds to hydrogen atoms, which last around 10 femtoseconds.

      The first MD simulation of a protein (McCammon, Gelin and Karplus, 1977) covered less than 10 picoseconds. In 2013 Martin Karplus, Michael Levitt and Arieh Warshel shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing multiscale models of complex chemical systems. Today, GPUs and special-purpose supercomputers such as Anton can reach microseconds to milliseconds.

      A photo and a movie

      In 2020, the AI program AlphaFold showed it could predict many protein structures from their sequence with remarkable accuracy. In 2024 its creators, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with David Baker, who designs brand-new proteins.

      A predicted structure is like a photo. But proteins are never still: they wobble, flex, open and close, and grab other molecules. Molecular dynamics gives us the movie, showing how a protein moves, how a medicine might bind to it, and what changes when a mutation swaps one amino acid for another.

      Designing antibiotics

      Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria or stop them growing. Most work by jamming one of the bacteria's own proteins. Penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928, blocks the enzymes bacteria use to build their cell walls, so the bacteria burst.

      A good antibiotic has to do three things:

      • Stick tightly in a pocket of a protein the bacteria need.
      • Leave our own proteins alone, so it doesn't cause side effects.
      • Keep working when the bacteria evolve.

      That last one is getting harder. Bacteria multiply fast, and a lucky mutation that changes one amino acid in a drug's target can stop the drug fitting. Other bacteria make enzymes that cut antibiotics apart. Drug-resistant infections directly caused about 1.14 million deaths worldwide in 2021, and researchers forecast nearly 39 million more between 2025 and 2050 unless we act.

      Computers help at every step. Scientists can search millions of molecules on screen (virtual screening), predict how each one sits in a pocket (docking), then use molecular dynamics to check whether it really stays put while the protein wobbles, and how a resistance mutation changes the fit. You can try this in the antibiotic lab.

      For the scientists

      MD is used to estimate binding free energies (for example with free energy perturbation), to measure how long a drug stays bound (its residence time), and to find “cryptic” pockets that only open as the protein moves. Selectivity can come down to small differences: the antibiotic trimethoprim binds bacterial dihydrofolate reductase thousands of times more tightly than the human enzyme. Resistance can also be tackled with combinations: co-amoxiclav pairs amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, which blocks the β-lactamase enzymes that would destroy it. Machine learning has joined the toolkit too: halicin (2020) and abaucin (2023) were found by AI models trained to spot molecules that kill bacteria.

      The antibiotic lab in this game is a 2D toy: three pocket amino acids, three-part molecules and simple oily, charged and polar interactions. Real binding depends on 3D shape, hydrogen-bond geometry, displacing water from the pocket and much more.

      When folding goes wrong

      Sometimes proteins misfold, or clump together into sticky tangles. Misfolded proteins are involved in Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease and cystic fibrosis. In the most common form of cystic fibrosis, a single missing amino acid stops a protein called CFTR from folding properly. Some modern cystic fibrosis medicines help it fold.

      Cells have helper proteins called chaperones that help chains fold correctly. Understanding how folding works, and how it fails, helps scientists design new medicines.

      For the scientists

      Alzheimer's disease involves aggregates of amyloid-β and tau; Parkinson's involves α-synuclein. In prion diseases such as Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, a misfolded form of the prion protein converts healthy copies into the misfolded form. The common cystic fibrosis mutation, F508del, causes CFTR to misfold and be degraded; “corrector” drugs help it fold and reach the cell surface.
